What Timescapes Should Say

This is a hypothetical redesign of the Timescapes homepage, built as a work sample for my application to the Senior Product Marketing Manager role in Vancouver.

The current homepage is very good at explaining what Timescapes is, but I think it should do more. It should explain why customers choose Timescapes and who its customer becomes: the jobsite hero who delivers a build faster, safer, and smarter. This is someone with mud on their boots and a spreadsheet open on the other screen. They don't care about reality capture; they simply want the job to move faster, they want nobody hurt, and they want to not lose the argument at the end of it.

So the page is rebuilt around reaching that customer quickly, using language they already use: faster, safer, smarter. Each is a link into the section that proves it.

Every metric, customer name and quotation below is drawn from timescapes.co as published. I re-worked existing copy and evidence that is currently under-used; I have not invented a product claim anywhere on this page. The imagery, colour and typography are Timescapes' own, augmented with a single orange accent that marks what is new.

The pink blocks throughout are my notes on why each change was made. The closing section sets out how I would take this from a homepage into a full go-to-market motion. All of it is built from public material, so with win/loss data and a week of customer calls some of it would change. That is the first work, not a caveat. This is a good indication of How I'd start.

Build faster

Decisions at site speed, from wherever you happen to be standing.

  • Check deliveries, subcontractor activity and progress against programme without making a phone call or walking the site.
  • Arrive on site already knowing what you came for. Fewer visits, and the ones you make are focused.
  • Assess real progress against your schedule before the weekly meeting, not during it.
  • Continuous automated capture takes the place of manual progress tracking.

Build safer

See the near miss, not just the incident report.

  • Review near misses and incidents in high-resolution footage, identify root causes, and swiftly implement corrective measures.
  • Verify immediately that health and safety protocols are in place and being followed, whether the site manager is on site or off.
  • Keep watch over live environments, from a fully operational airport to an electrified rail corridor.
  • Coordinate daily planning with your crew off current site images, so everyone is working from the same picture of today.

Build smarter

Win the argument before anyone starts having it.

  • Substantiate progress or delay claims with time-stamped visual evidence.
  • Confirm that deliveries and installations have been carried out correctly.
  • Overlay real site imagery against the 3D design model to catch clashes between what was drawn and what was built.
  • Give owners, investors and the public a single visual source of truth, rather than a version per stakeholder.

Where this goes next

A homepage is the visible end of a positioning system. Here is the rest of it.

Everything above is one artefact. What follows is how I would turn it into a go-to-market motion, an enablement programme, and a product marketing function that outlives any single page.

01 The character

Everything above is written for one person.

They are the technology or delivery lead on a Tier 1 or Tier 2 general contractor's project. The job title moves around, from Technology Systems Lead to Digital Engineering Manager to Senior Project Manager, but the shape doesn't. They have mud on their boots in the morning and a spreadsheet open in the afternoon. They are measured on programme and margin. They are exposed on two things they cannot fully control: whether the job runs to schedule, and whether anyone gets hurt.

They are not a buyer in the procurement sense. They are a specifier. They decide this project will be run with Timescapes on it, they defend that decision internally, and when the job lands well they are the reason it did. Timescapes' real customer is the person who chose it.

Christian McCartney at Hawkins is this character, on Timescapes' own website, in their own words: an operational asset that drives efficiency, collaboration and safety. That is not a testimonial, it is a persona document that already exists. Every asset the function produces should be written so this person recognises themselves inside one sentence.

Owners and developers are not a second universe requiring a parallel message. They are defined relative to the character: the people they have to keep informed, and the people who sometimes specify the tool before they do, as at Auckland Airport.

02 The positioning

Timescapes makes construction sites run faster, safer and smarter, by giving the people responsible for them a continuous visual record of what actually happened.

Three outcomes, one mechanism, one owner. That is the whole statement, and it is deliberately short enough that a rep can say it on a call without reading it.

Faster, safer and smarter are not headline words. They are a message architecture. Every asset the function ships inherits exactly one of the three and knows which one. A campaign, a release note, a battlecard, a conference banner, a partner announcement: each has a parent pillar. Anything that cannot name its parent is a signal that either the asset is unfocused or the architecture is missing something. It is a cheap test, it takes five seconds, and it is what keeps four markets and several channels saying the same thing without needing a committee to approve it.

The mechanism, continuous reality capture, does not disappear. It moves to where it belongs: the proof layer, not the promise.

03 What it becomes

The same three words flex across the business without being rewritten.

By segment

GCs rank faster first, because programme is what they get beaten up about. Owners and developers rank smarter first, because their exposure is the claim and the change order. Same architecture, different order, no new messaging to maintain.

By persona

The project lead wants to stop chasing updates, which is a faster story. The digital team wants integration, model overlay and data that survives handover, which is a smarter one. Both are already served by the pillars; they just need different doors into them.

By geography

Programme or schedule. Site or jobsite. H&S or EHS. The vocabulary changes across NZ, Australia, Canada and the US. The pillars do not.

By motion

This is the part a homepage cannot solve on its own. Cameras deploy per project, which means a won deal is one project, not one account, and revenue tracks project starts rather than seat growth. The real growth engine is becoming a contractor's default across their whole portfolio. That is a standardisation argument, aimed higher than the specifier, and it needs its own assets rather than borrowing the acquisition ones. Auckland Airport specifying Timescapes to Hawkins is that motion running in reverse, from the owner down, and it is the most repeatable story on the site.

The enablement that falls out of it

Battlecards keyed to each pillar, so a rep knows which argument they are actually in. An objection guide built from the objections that measurably stall deals, not the ones we imagine. An ROI calculator that runs on the character's own numbers: site visits avoided, claim hours saved, days of programme protected. Demo scripts that open on the outcome rather than the camera. One-pagers per segment. Case studies written so the character sees themselves in the first paragraph. And all of it on a maintenance cadence, because enablement built once and abandoned is worse than none. It teaches the sales team not to look.

04 How the function grows

Audit before building

Sit on calls, read win/loss, ask five reps what they wish they had walking into a deal. The asset list above is what I would expect to build; the order comes from where deals actually stall, and I would not commit to it before I know. If deals are consistently won on claims defence, smarter leads and the section order on this very page changes.

Ship one launch properly

Take the next release through the whole motion end to end, covering positioning, enablement, launch plan and measurement, and let that become the template. A working example beats a playbook document that nobody opens.

Then the compounding work

Competitive monitoring, customer research synthesis, first-draft content, and enablement generation are the four places AI does genuine work in this function. Built as durable workflows rather than one-off prompts, they are what makes it possible for one person to hold positioning across four markets without becoming the bottleneck.

This page was built that way. I used AI assistance for research synthesis, competitive scanning and drafting. The positioning calls are mine. The judgement is the job; the speed is the tooling.

Coach, don't police

The regional marketing managers already own their channels and know their markets. What they need from product marketing is the architecture, the words, and a fast answer when they ask which pillar something belongs to. Not an approval queue.

The thing I would check first

Whether "safer" survives contact with legal. Selling safety outcomes on a monitoring product raises liability questions and worker-surveillance questions, and it is simultaneously the pillar with the most upside and the most exposure. I would pressure-test it with a customer's H&S lead and with counsel before it went anywhere near a hero.
