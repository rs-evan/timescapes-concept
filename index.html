What Timescapes Should Say

This is a hypothetical redesign of the Timescapes homepage, built as a work sample for my application to the Senior Product Marketing Manager role in Vancouver.

The current homepage is very good at explaining what Timescapes is, but I think it should do more. It should explain why customers choose Timescapes and who its customer becomes: the jobsite hero who delivers a build faster, safer, and smarter. This is someone with mud on their boots and a spreadsheet open on the other screen. They don't care about reality capture; they simply want the job to move faster, they want nobody hurt, and they want to not lose the argument at the end of it.

So the page is rebuilt around reaching that customer quickly, using language they already use: faster, safer, smarter. Each is a link into the section that proves it.

Every metric, customer name and quotation below is drawn from timescapes.co as published. I re-worked existing copy and evidence that is currently under-used; I have not invented a product claim anywhere on this page. The imagery, color and typography are Timescapes' own, augmented with a single orange accent that marks what is new.

The pink blocks throughout are my notes on why each change was made. The closing section sets out how I would take this from a homepage into a full go-to-market motion. All of it is built from public material, so with win/loss data and a week of customer calls some of it would change. That is the first work, not a caveat. This is a good indication of How I'd start.

Build faster

Decisions at site speed, from wherever you happen to be standing.

  • Check deliveries, subcontractor activity and progress against schedule without making a phone call or walking the site.
  • Arrive on site already knowing what you came for. Fewer visits, and the ones you make are focused.
  • Assess real progress against your schedule before the weekly meeting, not during it.
  • Continuous automated capture takes the place of manual progress tracking.

Build safer

See the near miss, not just the incident report.

  • Review near misses and incidents in high-resolution footage, identify root causes, and swiftly implement corrective measures.
  • Verify immediately that health and safety protocols are in place and being followed, whether the site manager is on site or off.
  • Keep watch over live environments, from a fully operational airport to an electrified rail corridor.
  • Coordinate daily planning with your crew off current site images, so everyone is working from the same picture of today.

Build smarter

Win the argument before anyone starts having it.

  • Substantiate progress or delay claims with time-stamped visual evidence.
  • Confirm that deliveries and installations have been carried out correctly.
  • Overlay real site imagery against the 3D design model to catch clashes between what was drawn and what was built.
  • Give owners, investors and the public a single visual source of truth, rather than a version per stakeholder.

Where this goes next

What happens after the homepage.

Everything above is one page. Here's how I'd turn it into a go-to-market motion, an enablement program, and a product marketing function that outlives it.

01 The character

Everything above is written for one person.

They're the technology or delivery lead at a Tier 1 or Tier 2 general contractor. Titles move around: Technology Systems Lead, Digital Engineering Manager, Senior Project Manager. The job is the same. Muddy boots in the morning, spreadsheet in the afternoon. Measured on schedule and margin, and exposed on two things they can't fully control: whether the job runs late, and whether anyone gets hurt.

They don't sign the contract. They decide the project runs with Timescapes on it, defend that internally, and take the credit when it lands well.

McCartney at Hawkins is this person, on Timescapes' own site, in their own words. Every asset we make should be written so this person recognizes themselves in the first sentence.

Owners and developers fit the same story. They're the people our character keeps informed, and sometimes, like at Auckland Airport, the people who tell them what to use.

02 The positioning

Timescapes makes construction sites run faster, safer and smarter, by giving the people responsible for them a continuous visual record of what actually happened.

Three outcomes, one mechanism, one owner. Short enough that a rep can say it on a call without reading it.

Faster, safer and smarter then work as a filing system. Every campaign, release note, battlecard and conference banner belongs to one of the three, and we should be able to say which. When something won't file, that usually means the asset is unfocused. It takes five seconds to check, and it's what keeps four markets saying the same thing without a committee.

Continuous reality capture stays. It moves down a level, into the proof.

03 What it becomes

By segment

GCs put faster first, because schedule is what they get beaten up about. Owners and developers put smarter first, because their exposure is the claim. Same three words, different order.

By persona

The project lead wants to stop chasing updates. The digital team wants integration, model overlay, and data that survives handover. Both are already covered by the pillars. They need different ways in.

By geography

Schedule or programme. Jobsite or site. EHS or H&S. The vocabulary changes across NZ, Australia, Canada and the US. The three pillars don't.

By motion

Cameras go onto projects, so a won deal is one project and revenue tracks project starts. The bigger prize is becoming a contractor's default across their whole portfolio. That's a different argument, aimed at someone more senior, and it needs its own material rather than the acquisition material with the logo swapped. Auckland Airport telling Hawkins to use Timescapes is that motion running from the owner down, and it's the most repeatable story on the site.

What we build from it

Battlecards per pillar, so a rep knows which argument they are in. An objection guide built from the objections that measurably stall deals. An ROI calculator that runs on our character's numbers: site visits avoided, claim hours saved, days of schedule protected. Demo scripts that open on the outcome. One-pagers per segment. Case studies where the customer sees themselves in the first paragraph. And a maintenance cadence, because enablement nobody updates stops getting opened.

04 How the function grows

Audit first

Sit on calls, read win/loss, ask five reps what they wish they had walking into a deal. I expect to build the list above. The order comes from where deals actually stall, and I don't know that yet. If most deals turn on claims defense, smarter leads and this page changes.

Ship one launch properly

Take the next release through the whole motion, from positioning to enablement to launch plan to measurement, and use it as the template. I'd rather leave behind a worked example than a playbook.

Then the compounding work

Competitive monitoring, customer research synthesis, first drafts, enablement generation. Four places AI does real work in this function, built as workflows rather than one-off prompts. This page was built that way. I used AI for research and drafting, and made the positioning calls myself.

Coach, don't police

The regional marketing managers know their markets and own their channels. What they need is the words, and a fast answer when they ask which pillar something belongs to.

What I'd check first

Whether "safer" survives legal. Selling safety outcomes on a monitoring product raises liability questions and worker-surveillance questions, and it's the pillar with the most upside and the most exposure. I'd test it with a customer's health and safety lead and with counsel before it goes anywhere near a hero.
